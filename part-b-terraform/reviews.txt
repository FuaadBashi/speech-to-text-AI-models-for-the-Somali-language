Review #245A
===========================================================================

Overall merit
-------------
1. Reject

Reviewer expertise
------------------
3. Knowledgeable

Paper summary
-------------
This paper evaluates ClinicalBERT, BioBERT, and RoBERTa-Large models for clinical PHI de-identification on the i2b2 dataset. It argues that aggregate token-level F1 obscures entity-specific privacy risks, and therefore, it proposes the use of strict-typed entity exact-match as the primary metric and introduces a heuristic risk-weighted F1 score. A large portion of the paper reports and discusses detailed per-entity error and bias analyses.

Strengths (Reasons to accept)
-----------------------------
- Topic is good fit for CODASPY. 
- The problem considered is interesting and valuable (privacy of clinical data).
- F1 can indeed hide failures that matter operationally. 
- Detailed, per-entity-type analyses are indeed valuable. Showing that some entities like LOCATION/PHONE are especially affected is valuable.

Weaknesses (Reasons to reject)
------------------------------
- Limited methodological and scientific novelty (paper is mostly experimentation).
- Experiment scope is limited and outdated (only i2b2 dataset from 2006).
- Risk weighting (e.g., Table 5) seems heuristic and only for the data in hand -- which hurts the generalizability of the results.
- Quality of writing can be improved.

Comments for authors
--------------------
I believe that the paper's goals and approach are valuable; however, the novelty and scope are currently too narrow for conference-level publication. 

First, regarding novelty: The paper is focused on experimentation and discussion; it does not introduce new datasets, de-identification methods, or models. As such, the scientific novelty is limited. The experimental findings that token-level F1 inflates performance and entity-level exact-match is stricter are OK, but they are not too novel. Furthermore, some findings (e.g., LOCATION being the hardest PHI category) could be due to the datasets/models in hand.

Second, continuing from the last point: In order for the results to be generalizable, the paper should have tested on more datasets, and perhaps more recent and specialized PII detection models. The experiments rely on a single dataset (i2b2 2006), which raises doubts about the generalizability of the paper's findings and impact.

The paper advocates for a risk-weighted evaluation metric (customized F1) but the choice of weights seems overly dependent on the data / use case that is being considered. Again, I worry about the generalizability and scientific impact of findings that depend on a narrow experimental scope and heuristic set of weights.

Some other minor issues exist, e.g., some plots like Figure 7 and 8 are difficult to read, and some citations in the text are missing, e.g., [?]. The paper's presentation can be improved.

Overall merit
-------------
1. Reject

Reviewer expertise

2. Some familiarity

Paper summary
-------------
This paper provides a risk-weighted, entity-level analysis of transformer-based approaches for clinical PHI (Protected Health Information) de-identification. The experimental evaluation compares models performing at a high-level standard evaluation metrics and demonstrates variation within these metrics. The work argues that these results motivate a more entity-specific and risk-aware evaluation approach to transformer-based de-identification. The paper positions the contribution as advancing privacy-preserving clinical NLP by aligning evaluation metrics with privacy risk. While the problem of PHI de-identification is practically important, the paper’s framing as a privacy research contribution is weak. The technical novelty is limited given the relatively dated the privacy model and the evaluation framework does not meaningfully engage with modern privacy notions or adversarial risk. Additionally, nearly all cited work is from medical or health focused venues rather than the privacy or security literature.

Strengths (Reasons to accept)
-----------------------------
- Relevance of privacy to the clinical setting: clinical de-identification remains operationally important for hospital data release workflows. Improving transformer-based workflows for PHI tagging may have practical value in applied health informatics contexts.

- Experimental evaluation: the experimental pipeline considers multiple models and provides a lens through which standard metrics should be reevaluated.

- Data consistent with the proposed setting is leveraged in experimentation. (Though notable that citations appear to be missing)

Weaknesses (Reasons to reject)
------------------------------
The weaknesses are substantial in the context of this venue.

- Outdated privacy framing: contemporary privacy research has largely moved beyond syntactic redaction approaches toward: formal privacy definitions (e.g., differential privacy), adversarial inference risk modeling, end-to-end privacy-preserving methods. Analysis of de-identification processes, though perhaps remaining practically relevant, does not meaningfully engage with modern privacy threat models as would be expected from work at this venue.

- Nearly all citations are from clinical NLP, bioinformatics, or medical informatics venues. There is little to no engagement with the privacy literature.

- Risk evaluation does not engage with modern privacy attacks or inference risks.

Comments for authors
--------------------
While the operational problem of PHI tagging is relevant in clinical NLP, the paper does speak to audience targeting privacy and security research. The framing equates de-identification accuracy with privacy protection, which is not supported by modern privacy theory or empirical attack literature. Substantial reframing and deeper engagement with privacy models would be required for this work to be suitable for a privacy-focused venue.

This paper provides an empirical comparison of various transformer models for PII detection in natural language clinical records.

Strengths (Reasons to accept)
-----------------------------
1. This is a comprehensive evaluation than what has been performed to date.

2. The experimental analysis is well constructured and executed.

3. Thge paper is generally easy to read and the concepts are easy to follow

Weaknesses (Reasons to reject)
------------------------------
1. There is not much novelty in this work from a computational perspective.  This work may be more appropriate for a medical informatics conference (or journal) than for a CS venue.

2. Given that the wold is increasingly transitioning to large language models for clinical text processing, the paper would benefit from a discussion on why transformer models are still of relevance for this problem.

3. The reviewer recognizes the lack of public natural language clinical datasets that are available, but this is a relatively old dataset now and it is unclear how these results scale to actual medical records de-identification problems.
